If a non-physicist friend asks "what do you actually measure?", here's the version that fits on a napkin.

## Two heavy nuclei walk into a collider

At RHIC we slam gold nuclei together at 200 GeV per nucleon-pair. For a moment — about a yoctosecond
(10⁻²⁴ s) — the energy density is so high that protons and neutrons melt into a soup of quarks and gluons.
We call this state the **quark–gluon plasma (QGP)**.

We cannot see the plasma directly. It's too small, too fast, too short-lived. Instead, we watch what
**comes out** of the fireball after it cools.

## Jets as probes

When two quarks scatter hard early in the collision, they produce a pair of high-momentum partons that
shower into a collimated spray of hadrons. We call that spray a **jet**.

The trick: the jet is **born inside the plasma** and travels out through it. The plasma takes energy
away from the jet — softens it, broadens it, splits it. By comparing jets in:

- **p+p collisions** (no plasma, just QCD)
- **Au+Au collisions** (plasma is there)

...we learn how the plasma interacts with very energetic quarks and gluons. This is called
**jet quenching**.

## Why D⁰-tagged jets are special

A D⁰ meson contains a **charm quark**. Charm is much heavier than the light quarks (u, d, s), so it's
produced early in the collision and "remembers" the early stage. A jet tagged with a D⁰ tells us about
how the plasma affects a heavy parton — different from light-quark or gluon jets.

In current STAR analyses we measure D⁰-tagged jet substructure with an AI-based unfolding method called
[OmniFold](https://arxiv.org/abs/1911.09107), which lets us correct multi-dimensional distributions
without binning everything.

## The picture

> A jet enters a hot QCD soup, loses energy and structure to it, and we measure
> *how much, where, in which direction*.

That's it. Everything else is detector calibration, statistics, and a lot of patience.
